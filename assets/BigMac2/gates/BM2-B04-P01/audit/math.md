## 1. Reconstruction of the invariant-family bound

Every matrix fixed by the indicated (S_4) action has the form

\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}
1&a&b&b&b&b\\
a&1&c&c&c&c\\
b&c&1&d&d&d\\
b&c&d&1&d&d\\
b&c&d&d&1&d\\
b&c&d&d&d&1
\end{pmatrix},\qquad a,b,c,d>0.
\]

On the three-dimensional contrast space in the last four coordinates, (P)
acts by (1-d).  On its orthogonal complement it is represented by

\[
Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&a&2b\\a&1&2c\\2b&2c&s\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s=1+3d.
\]

Direct expansion gives

\[
\det Q=(1-a^2)(s-4b^2)-4(c-ab)^2.
\]

The condition that (P) have at most one positive eigenvalue is therefore
equivalent, within (a,b,c,d>0), to

\[
d\geq1,\qquad a\geq1,\qquad 4b^2\geq s,
\qquad (1-a^2)(s-4b^2)\geq4(c-ab)^2. \tag{1}
\]

For necessity, the contrast eigenvalues give (d\geq1); interlacing on the
((1,2)) and ((1,3)) principal blocks gives (a\geq1) and (4b^2\geq s);
and a (3\times3) matrix with its unique positive eigenvalue and two
nonpositive eigenvalues has nonnegative determinant.  For sufficiency when
(a>1), the leading (2\times2) block has one eigenvalue of each sign.
Interlacing fixes the largest and smallest eigenvalues of (Q) as positive and
negative, and (det Q\geq0) forces the middle eigenvalue to be nonpositive.
When (a=1), (1) forces (c=b); the vector ((1,-1,0)) is null and the
remaining block has determinant (2(s-4b^2)\leq0).  This also covers all
singular boundary cases allowed by the problem.

The target monomial is

\[
F_\alpha(P)=\frac{\sqrt a\,d^{3/2}}{b^2c}. \tag{2}
\]

Set (a=\cosh x) and (2b/\sqrt{s}=\cosh y), uniquely with (x,y\geq0).
The last inequality in (1) implies

\[
c\geq ab-\frac12\sqrt{(a^2-1)(4b^2-s)}
=\frac{\sqrt{s}}2\cosh(x-y). \tag{3}
\]

Consequently

\[
F_\alpha(P)\leq 8\left(\frac d{s}\right)^{3/2}
\frac{\sqrt{\cosh x}}{\cosh^2y\,\cosh(x-y)}. \tag{4}
\]

I checked the last factor independently.  Put (z=x-y),
(p=\tanh y\), and (q=\tanh z).  Squaring the desired estimate reduces it to

\[
1+pq\leq \cosh^3y\cosh z.
\]

If (q\leq0), the left side is at most (1) and the right side is at least
(1).  If (q\geq0), Cauchy--Schwarz gives

\[
\sqrt{(1-p^2)(1-q^2)}+pq\leq1,
\]

which implies (1+pq\leq\cosh y\cosh z\leq\cosh^3y\cosh z).  Thus the
factor in (4) is at most one.  Since (d/(1+3d)<1/3),

\[
F_\alpha(P)<\frac8{3\sqrt3}<2.
\]

For every (d\geq1), the choice

\[
a=1,\qquad b=c=\frac{\sqrt{1+3d}}2
\]

satisfies (1) and gives
(F_\alpha(P)=8(d/(1+3d))^{3/2}).  Sending (d\to\infty) proves that the
supremum is exactly (8/(3\sqrt3)).  Positivity and the permitted singular
cases were not lost in this limiting argument.

## 2. Reconstruction of the geometric-symmetrization obstruction

For (r>1), split the indices into
(A=\{1,5,6\}) and (B=\{2,3,4\}), use entry (1) within either part and
entry (r) across the parts.  The four within-part contrast vectors are null,
and on the two part-constant vectors the matrix is

\[
\begin{pmatrix}3&3r\\3r&3\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its inertia is exactly ((1,1,4)), so it belongs to the original domain.
Orbit counting under permutations of (3,4,5,6) gives geometric-average
parameters

\[
a=r,\qquad b=c=\sqrt r,\qquad d=r^{2/3}.
\]

The three contrast eigenvalues are (1-r^{2/3}<0), and the vector
((1,-1,0)) in the (Q)-block has eigenvalue (1-r<0).  The remaining block
is

\[
B_r=\begin{pmatrix}1+r&2\sqrt{2r}\\2\sqrt{2r}&1+3r^{2/3}\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Writing (x=r^{1/3}>1), direct expansion yields

\[
\det B_r=(x-1)^2(3x^3+6x^2+2x+1)>0.
\]

Both diagonal entries are positive, so (B_r) is positive definite and the
geometric average has inertia ((2,4,0)).  At (r=8), I also checked directly
the exact nonzero spectrum ({27,-21\}) of the original matrix and the
average's eigenvalues (11\pm2\sqrt{17},-7,-3,-3,-3).

Finally, the exponent sum on the cross edges of the displayed cut is

\[
\frac14\left(\sum_{i\in A}h_i\right)
\left(\sum_{j\in B}h_j\right)=\frac14(0)(1)=0.
\]

Hence both the original matrix and its geometric average have target monomial
equal to (1).  This is exactly a counterexample to domain closure, not to the
desired six-point inequality.

## 3. Reconstruction of the product-proof-cone obstruction

Every matrix in the domain has (p_{ij}\geq1): otherwise its corresponding
(2\times2) principal submatrix would have two positive eigenvalues, contrary
to interlacing.  Thus (p_{ij}^{-1}\leq1) supplies the exponent generator
(-e_{ij}).

For an exponent vector (v), define

\[
\Lambda(v)=3v_{12}+\sum_{k=3}^6v_{1k}
+2\sum_{k=3}^6v_{2k}
+2\sum_{3\leq i<j\leq6}v_{ij}.
\]

All coefficients are positive, so (Lambda(-e_{ij})<0).  Direct substitution
gives (Lambda(q(h))=2), hence
(Lambda(\alpha)=1/2>0).  I independently enumerated the unique permutations
of ((1,1,-1,0,0,0)) and ((1,1,1,-1,-1,0)), rather than relying only on the
provided enumeration loops.  Exact integer arithmetic produced

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\Lambda(q_T)&-4&-2&0\\ \hline
\text{count}&4&46&10
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
\Lambda(q_P)&-6&-4&-2&0\\ \hline
\text{count}&12&28&16&4.
\end{array}
\]

The supplied checker produces the same histograms.  Therefore every
nonnegative real combination of triangular generators (q_T), normalized
pentagonal generators (q_P/2), and (-e_{ij}) has nonpositive
(Lambda), while the target has positive (Lambda).  No such product
certificate exists.  The argument does not use the usual total-weight
restriction, so it proves the stronger stated exclusion.
