A STRICT CERTIFIED SEARCH EXTENSION FOR ERDŐS
PROBLEM 647
ZIJIAN ZENG

Abstract. Let τ (m) denote the number of positive divisors of m. Erdős
Problem 647 asks whether there is an n > 24 for which maxm<n (m + τ (m)) ≤
n + 2. We give a reproducible finite exclusion for
9,174,471,185,880,000,000 < n ≤ 9,180,628,549,092,000,000.
It reconstructs all 96 residue classes in the published theorem-grade modular reduction, expands them into 50,784 arithmetic progressions, and checks
5,078,450,784 progression cells. Exact small-prime divisor lower bounds reject
all but 21 cells; those 21 are factored independently and each violates one
of the shifts 1 ≤ k ≤ 6. Combined with the reported previous computation
through the left endpoint, this gives a strict new computational frontier at
9,180,628,549,092,000,000. The inherited prefix was not rerun here, and the
global existence problem remains open.

1. Introduction
For a positive integer m, write τ (m) for its number of positive divisors. The
problem, originating with Erdős and Selfridge, asks whether there is an integer
n > 24 such that

The value 24 is the last known equality case [2, 1]. Direct and certificate-based finite
computations have successively treated ranges through 1012 and beyond [5, 6, 7],
but no finite computation alone settles the existential question. Erdős also noted
that a related fixed-window statement follows from Schinzel’s Hypothesis H [3]; that
conditional statement does not control the full prefix in (1).

The distinction is important: Theorem 3.1 is supported by the new local certificate,
whereas Corollary 3.2 additionally inherits the earlier computation.

4. Certificate and independent replay
The serialized input records the endpoints, the formula (4), the full Cartesian
product R × {0, . . . , 528}, and cryptographic hashes. The verifier does not accept a
copied residue list: it regenerates R from (3), proves that its size is 96, and checks
that the 50,784 rows are exactly the required Cartesian product. It also checks
the identities defining A and the two endpoints and refuses arithmetic outside the
signed 64-bit domain.

5. Scope and disclosure
This result is a strict finite bound, not a proof that a second n exists or does
not exist. Large unsuccessful searches cannot decide the remaining infinite tail. A
second novelty search on August 29, 2026 found no publication or database entry
reporting the exact endpoint above; this is a bounded search statement, not a claim
about unpublished work.
The new replay used no proof assistant. Its imported modular prerequisite was
formalized in Lean 4 by Hughes; the present project inspected that theorem boundary
but did not invoke Lean locally. OpenAI Codex assisted with literature search,
proof exploration, code, tests, and prose. The discovery scan used an existing C
program [7]; the decisive replay was separately implemented and used exact integer
arithmetic. Runs were deterministic and used no random seed.
