 Abstract

 The subtree polynomial of a tree is the generating function that
enumerates its subtrees according to their orders. Brown and Mol
conjectured that every subtree root of a tree of order n\geq 2
lies in the disk

 \left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1+\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}\right\}. 

 We confirm this conjecture. We also show that every nonzero subtree
root z satisfies

 |z|>\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}-1. 

 Consequently, all nonzero subtree roots lie in the annular region

 \left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}-1<|z|\leq 1+\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}\right\}. 

 The upper bound is sharp for every even n and is
asymptotically sharp as n\to\infty through odd integers.
The lower bound is also asymptotically sharp as n\to\infty.

 Keywords: 

subtree polynomial; subtree root

 05C31

 1 Introduction

 Let T be a tree of order n, and let s_{k}(T) denote the number of
subtrees of T with k vertices.
The subtree polynomial of T, introduced by Jamison in [ 2 ] ,
is

 \Phi_{T}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}(T)x^{k}. 

 Subtree enumeration and the distribution of subtree orders have been studied
from several perspectives, including extremal enumeration, average subtree
order, and local subtree statistics; see, for example,
 [ 2 , 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] .
The zeros of \Phi_{T} are called subtree roots .

 In [ 4 ] , Brown and Mol established a universal upper bound on the moduli
of subtree roots.

 Theorem 1.1 . 

 [ 4 ] 
The subtree roots of every tree T lie in the disk 

 \{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1+\sqrt[3]{3}\}, 

 and the only tree with a subtree root on the boundary of this disk is K_{1,3}. 

 Brown and Mol further proposed an order-dependent refinement of Theorem 1.1 .

 Conjecture 1.2 . 

 [ 4 ] 
Let T be a tree of order n\geq 2. Then the subtree roots of T lie in the disk 

 \{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1+\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}\}. 

 For n\geq 4, the radius in Conjecture 1.2 is strictly decreasing
with n, so the
inductive argument used for the universal disk cannot be applied directly:
passing to a proper subtree changes the target radius in the wrong direction.
To overcome this difficulty, we introduce a recursively defined quantity that
controls, at a fixed modulus, the ratio between the subtree polynomial of a
vertex-deleted forest and the local subtree polynomial at the deleted vertex.
A sharp estimate for this ratio at the conjectured radius then yields the
desired zero-free region.

 Our main result confirms this conjecture.

 Theorem 1.3 . 

 Let T be a tree of order n\geq 2. Then the subtree roots of T lie in the disk 

 \{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1+\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}\}. 

 The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we develop the
local decomposition formulas and the recursive comparison estimate needed for
the proof of Theorem  1.3 .
In Section 3, we shall prove Theorem  1.3 . In Section 4, we provide a lower bound on the modulus of nonzero complex subtree roots and discuss the sharpness of our upper and lower bounds. In the final section, we recall a stronger conjecture of Brown and
Mol that implies Conjecture 1.2 .

 2 Preliminaries

 We first list several related definitions and observations on the subtree polynomial which have appeared in the literature. In [ 4 ] , the definition of subtree polynomial was extended to forests, as forests were regarded as vertex-deleted subgraphs of trees. If F is a forest with components T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}, then the subtree polynomial of F is given by

 \varPhi_{F}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varPhi_{T_{i}}(x). 

 Let T be a tree of order n, and let v\in V(T).
For each k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}, let s_{k}(T,v) denote the number of subtrees of T of order k containing v. Then the local subtree polynomial of T at v, introduced in [ 2 ] , is defined by

 \varPhi_{T,v}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}(T,v)x^{k}. 

 For any vertex u of a tree T, every subtree of T either contains u or is contained in T-u.
Consequently,

 \Phi_{T}(x)=\Phi_{T,u}(x)+\Phi_{T-u}(x). 

 (2.1) 

 Let T_{1},\dots,T_{d} be the components of T-u, where d is the degree of the vertex u in T, and let u_{i} be the
unique neighbor of u in T_{i}. A subtree containing u is obtained by
choosing, independently for each i, either no vertex of T_{i} or a
subtree of T_{i} containing u_{i}. Hence

 \Phi_{T,u}(x)=x\prod_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(1+\Phi_{T_{i},u_{i}}(x)\bigr). 

 (2.2) 

 We shall use the following lower bound given by Brown and Mol in [ 4 ] .

 Lemma 2.1 . 

 [ 4 ] 
Let T be a tree of order n, and let z\in\mathbb{C} with |z|\geq 2. Then for every vertex v of T,
|\Phi_{T,v}(z)|\geq|z|(|z|-1)^{n-1}. 

 In order to estimate |\Phi_{T-u}(z)|/|\Phi_{T,u}(z)| in the next section, we introduce several auxiliary functions and establish the required
monotonicity properties.

 Let c_{1},\dots,c_{d}>0, and define

 H_{\bm{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}x_{i}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}-1)}. 

 Lemma 2.2 . 

 If 1<x_{i}\leq y_{i} for every i=1,\dots,d, then 

 H_{\bm{c}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\leq H_{\bm{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}). 

 Moreover, the inequality is strict if there exists an index j such that x_{j}<y_{j}. 

 Proof.

 For each j, since 

 \frac{\partial H_{\bm{c}}}{\partial x_{j}}=-\frac{c_{j}+\sum_{i\neq j}c_{i}x_{i}}{(x_{j}-1)\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}-1)}<0, 

 it follows that H_{\bm{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is strictly decreasing in each variable x_{j} on (1,+\infty). 

 Set 

 \bm{w}_{0}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}), 

 and, for j=1,\dots,d, define 

 \bm{w}_{j}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{j},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{d}). 

 Clearly, 

 \bm{w}_{d}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}). 

 For each j, the vectors \bm{w}_{j-1} and
\bm{w}_{j} differ only in their j-th coordinate, and x_{j}\leq y_{j}. Since H_{\bm{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is strictly decreasing in each variable x_{j} on (1,\infty), we have 

 H_{\bm{c}}\bigl(\bm{w}_{j}\bigr)\leq H_{\bm{c}}\bigl(\bm{w}_{j-1}\bigr) 

 with strict inequality whenever x_{j}<y_{j}. Thus 

 H_{\bm{c}}\bigl(\bm{w}_{d}\bigr)\leq H_{\bm{c}}\bigl(\bm{w}_{d-1}\bigr)\leq\ldots\leq H_{\bm{c}}\bigl(\bm{w}_{0}\bigr) 

 This completes the proof.
∎ 

 For n\geq 1 and r\geq 2, define

 \lambda_{n}(r)=r(r-1)^{n-1}. 

 For a positive integer n, let \mathcal{C}(n) denote the set of all
compositions of n into positive integers. Define B_{1}(r)=0, and
for n\geq 2 define

 B_{n}(r)=\max_{(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in\mathcal{C}(n-1)}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)\bigl(1+B_{n_{i}}(r)\bigr)}{\displaystyle r\prod_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)-1\bigr)}. 

 This recursion is well defined because every part n_{i} is
smaller than n, the set \mathcal{C}(n-1) is finite, and
\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)-1\geq 1>0
for r\geq 2.
For illustration, the first two nontrivial functions in this recursion can be computed explicitly:

 B_{2}(r)=\frac{1}{r-1}, 

 and

 B_{3}(r)=\max\left\{\frac{r}{r^{2}-r-1},\frac{2}{(r-1)^{2}}\right\}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{2}{(r-1)^{2}},&2\leq r\leq 1+\sqrt{2},\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\frac{r}{r^{2}-r-1},&r\geq 1+\sqrt{2}.\end{cases} 

 Lemma 2.3 . 

 For every integer n\geq 2, the function B_{n}(r) is strictly decreasing in
r on [2,+\infty). 

 Proof.

 We use induction on n.
For n=2, 

 B_{2}(r)=\frac{1}{r-1}, 

 which is strictly decreasing for r\geq 2. 

 Now let n\geq 3, and assume that B_{j}(r) is strictly decreasing for every
2\leq j<n. Recall that B_{1}(r)=0, so B_{1}(r) is nonincreasing as well. 

 Fix a composition 

 \bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in\mathcal{C}(n-1), 

 and define 

 E_{\bm{n}}(r)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)\left(1+B_{n_{i}}(r)\right)}{\displaystyle r\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)-1\right)}. 

 We first prove that E_{\bm{n}}(r) is strictly decreasing. 

 Take two real numbers such that 2\leq r_{1}<r_{2}. For j\in\{1,2\}, write 

 x_{i}^{(j)}=\lambda_{n_{i}}(r_{j}), 

 and 

 c_{i}^{(j)}=1+B_{n_{i}}(r_{j}). 

 Since, for every integer k\geq 1, the function \lambda_{k}(r)=r(r-1)^{k-1}
is strictly increasing in r\in[2,\infty), we have x_{i}^{(1)}<x_{i}^{(2)}
for every i. By the induction hypothesis and the fact that B_{1}=0 when n_{i}=1,
we have c_{i}^{(2)}\leq c_{i}^{(1)}
for every i. 

 For a coefficient vector \bm{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{d}), write 

 H_{\bm{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}x_{i}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}-1)}. 

 Then 

 E_{\bm{n}}(r_{j})=\frac{1}{r_{j}}H_{\bm{c}^{(j)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(j)},\dots,x_{d}^{(j)}\right). 

 Note that x_{i}>1 for 1\leq i\leq d. The function H_{\bm{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}) is strictly increasing in each coefficient
c_{i}. Therefore 

 \displaystyle H_{\bm{c}^{(2)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(2)},\dots,x_{d}^{(2)}\right)\leq H_{\bm{c}^{(1)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(2)},\dots,x_{d}^{(2)}\right). 

 Since x_{i}^{(1)}<x_{i}^{(2)} for every i,
by Lemma  2.2 , we have 

 \displaystyle H_{\bm{c}^{(1)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(2)},\dots,x_{d}^{(2)}\right)<H_{\bm{c}^{(1)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(1)},\dots,x_{d}^{(1)}\right). 

 Then 

 H_{\bm{c}^{(2)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(2)},\dots,x_{d}^{(2)}\right)<H_{\bm{c}^{(1)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(1)},\dots,x_{d}^{(1)}\right). 

 Since 1/r_{2}<1/r_{1}, H_{\bm{c}^{(2)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(2)},\dots,x_{d}^{(2)}\right)\geq 0 and H_{\bm{c}^{(1)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(1)},\dots,x_{d}^{(1)}\right)\geq 0, we have 

 \displaystyle E_{\bm{n}}(r_{2})=\frac{1}{r_{2}}H_{\bm{c}^{(2)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(2)},\dots,x_{d}^{(2)}\right)<\frac{1}{r_{1}}H_{\bm{c}^{(1)}}\!\left(x_{1}^{(1)},\dots,x_{d}^{(1)}\right)=E_{\bm{n}}(r_{1}). 

 Thus, for every fixed composition
\bm{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n-1), the function E_{\bm{n}} is strictly decreasing. 

 Finally, choose a composition \bm{n}_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(n-1) for which
B_{n}(r_{2})=E_{\bm{n}_{2}}(r_{2}). Then 

 B_{n}(r_{1})\geq E_{\bm{n}_{2}}(r_{1})>E_{\bm{n}_{2}}(r_{2})=B_{n}(r_{2}). 

 Hence B_{n}(r) is strictly decreasing in
r on [2,+\infty).
∎ 

 For n\geq 5, define

 a_{n}=(n-1)^{1/(n-1)} 

 and

 \gamma_{n}=1+a_{n}. 

 We now establish two elementary inequalities involving a_{n}.

 Lemma 2.4 . 

 For every integer k with 2\leq k\leq n-1, one has a_{n}^{k}\leq k. 

 Proof.

 The function x^{1/x} is decreasing on [e,\infty). If
3\leq k\leq n-1, then 

 a_{n}=(n-1)^{1/(n-1)}\leq k^{1/k}, 

 and hence a_{n}^{k}\leq k. For k=2, since n-1\geq 4, 

 a_{n}=(n-1)^{1/(n-1)}\leq 4^{1/4}=\sqrt{2}, 

 so again a_{n}^{2}\leq 2.
∎ 

 For 1\leq k\leq n-1, define

 q_{k}=1+a_{n}^{-1}-a_{n}^{-k}. 

 Then q_{k}\geq 1, and

 (1+a)a_{n}^{k-1}-1=a_{n}^{k}q_{k}. 

 (2.3) 

 Lemma 2.5 . 

 Let m_{1},\dots,m_{d} be positive integers such that
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{d}=m\leq n-1. Then 

 \sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(a_{n}^{m_{i}-1}+m_{i}-1\bigr)\leq m\prod_{i=1}^{d}q_{m_{i}}. 

 Proof.

 We first show that 

 a_{n}^{k-1}+k-1\leq kq_{k}\qquad(1\leq k\leq n-1). 

 For k=1, equality holds. For k\geq 2, Lemma  2.4 gives 

 a_{n}^{k}\bigl(kq_{k}-a_{n}^{k-1}-k+1\bigr)=(k-a_{n}^{k})(a_{n}^{k-1}-1)\geq 0. 

 Therefore, 

 \sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(a_{n}^{m_{i}-1}+m_{i}-1\bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}q_{m_{i}}. 

 Since every q_{m_{i}}\geq 1, we have 

 \sum_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}q_{m_{i}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{d}q_{m_{j}}=m\prod_{j=1}^{d}q_{m_{j}}. 

 ∎ 

 We now combine the preceding two elementary inequalities to obtain the required upper bound for B_{m}(\gamma_{n}).

 Lemma 2.6 . 

 Let n be an integer with n\geq 5. Then, for each integer m with 1\leq m\leq n, 

 B_{m}(\gamma_{n})\leq\frac{m-1}{a_{n}^{m-1}}. 

 Proof.

 For a fixed n\geq 5, we use induction on m. The assertion is immediate for m=1. Let
m\geq 2, and assume the result for all smaller indices. Fix a composition
(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathcal{C}(m-1). Since
\lambda_{k}(\gamma_{n})=(1+a_{n})a_{n}^{k-1}, we, by the induction hypothesis, have 

 \lambda_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})\bigl(1+B_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})\bigr)\leq(1+a_{n})\bigl(a_{n}^{m_{i}-1}+m_{i}-1\bigr). 

 Therefore 

 \sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})\bigl(1+B_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})\bigr)\leq(1+a_{n})\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(a_{n}^{m_{i}-1}+m_{i}-1\bigr)\leq(1+a_{n})(m-1)\prod_{i=1}^{d}q_{m_{i}}, 

 where the last inequality holds by Lemma 2.5 .
By Equation ( 2.3 ) and the fact m_{1}+\cdots+m_{d}=m-1, 

 \prod_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(\lambda_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})-1\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\bigl((1+a_{n})a_{n}^{m_{i}-1}-1\bigr)=a_{n}^{m-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}q_{m_{i}}. 

 Therefore 

 \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})\bigl(1+B_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})\bigr)}{\displaystyle\gamma_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(\lambda_{m_{i}}(\gamma_{n})-1\bigr)}\leq\frac{(1+a_{n})(m-1)\prod_{i=1}^{d}q_{m_{i}}}{(1+a_{n})a_{n}^{m-1}\prod_{i=1}^{d}q_{m_{i}}}\leq\frac{m-1}{a_{n}^{m-1}}. 

 Taking the maximum over all compositions of m-1 proves the conclusion.
∎ 

 3 Proof of Theorem 1.3 

 The next lemma is the central comparison estimate.

 Lemma 3.1 . 

 Let T be a tree of order n, let u\in V(T), and let
z\in\mathbb{C} satisfy |z|=r\geq 2. Then 

 |\Phi_{T-u}(z)|\leq B_{n}(r)|\Phi_{T,u}(z)|. 

 Proof.

 We proceed by induction on n. If n=1, then T-u is empty and the
left-hand side is zero. 

 Assume n\geq 2. Let T_{1},\dots,T_{d} be the components of T-u, rooted
at the corresponding neighbors u_{1},\dots,u_{d} of u, and let
n_{i}=|V(T_{i})|. For convenience, set 

 L_{i}=\Phi_{T_{i},u_{i}}(z) 

 and 

 F_{i}=\Phi_{T_{i}-u_{i}}(z). 

 By the induction hypothesis, 

 |F_{i}|\leq B_{n_{i}}(r)|L_{i}|. 

 Since \Phi_{T_{i}}(z)=L_{i}+F_{i} and
\Phi_{T-u}(z)=\sum_{i}\Phi_{T_{i}}(z), we obtain 

 |\Phi_{T-u}(z)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(|F_{i}|+|L_{i}|\bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(1+B_{n_{i}}(r)\bigr)|L_{i}|. 

 On the other hand, the product formula ( 2.2 ) and the
reverse triangle inequality give 

 |\Phi_{T,u}(z)|=r\prod_{i=1}^{d}|1+L_{i}|\geq r\prod_{i=1}^{d}\bigl(|L_{i}|-1\bigr). 

 By Lemma  2.1 , we have 

 |L_{i}|\geq\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)>1, 

 and then |\Phi_{T,u}(z)|>0.
Consequently, 

 \frac{|\Phi_{T-u}(z)|}{|\Phi_{T,u}(z)|}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}|L_{i}|\bigl(1+B_{n_{i}}(r)\bigr)}{r\prod_{i=1}^{d}(|L_{i}|-1)}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)\bigl(1+B_{n_{i}}(r)\bigr)}{r\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)-1)}, 

 where the last inequality holds by Lemma  2.2 .
By the definition of
B_{n}(r), we have 

 \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)\bigl(1+B_{n_{i}}(r)\bigr)}{r\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\lambda_{n_{i}}(r)-1)}\leq B_{n}(r). 

 Thus 

 \frac{|\Phi_{T-u}(z)|}{|\Phi_{T,u}(z)|}\leq B_{n}(r). 

 This completes the proof. ∎ 

 We now prove Theorem  1.3 .

 Proof of Theorem  1.3 .

 For n=2, the only tree is P_{2}, and 

 \Phi_{P_{2}}(z)=z(z+2), 

 so the assertion is immediate. For n=3, the only tree is P_{3}, and 

 \Phi_{P_{3}}(z)=z(z^{2}+2z+3). 

 Its two nonzero roots have modulus \sqrt{3}<1+\sqrt{2}. For n=4, the
required radius is 1+\sqrt[3]{3}, so the result follows from
Theorem  1.1 . 

 Now assume that n\geq 5.
Let z\in\mathbb{C} satisfy |z|=r>\gamma_{n}, and choose any vertex
u\in V(T). Since \gamma_{n}>2, Lemma  3.1 gives 

 |\Phi_{T-u}(z)|\leq B_{n}(r)|\Phi_{T,u}(z)|. 

 By Lemmas 2.3 and 2.6 , 

 B_{n}(r)<B_{n}(\gamma_{n})\leq\frac{n-1}{a_{n}^{n-1}}=1. 

 Consequently, 

 |\Phi_{T-u}(z)|<|\Phi_{T,u}(z)|. 

 By Equation ( 2.1 ) and the reverse triangle inequality, we have 

 |\Phi_{T}(z)|\geq|\Phi_{T,u}(z)|-|\Phi_{T-u}(z)|>0. 

 Thus \Phi_{T}(z)\neq 0 whenever |z|>\gamma_{n}.
The proof is complete.
∎ 

 4 A lower bound and sharpness results

 To establish a lower bound on the modulus” of nonzero complex subtree roots, the following result is essential.

 Lemma 4.1 . 

 [ 2 ] 
Let T be a tree of order n. Then s_{1}(T)=n and for k=2,\ldots,n, 

 n-k+1\leq s_{k}(T)\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}. 

 We now give the lower bound on the modulus” of nonzero complex subtree roots.

 Theorem 4.2 . 

 Let T be a tree of order n\geq 3. Then every nonzero subtree root of T satisfies 

 |z|>\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}-1. 

 Proof.

 It suffices to prove that for 0<|z|\leq\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}-1, 

 \left|\frac{\varPhi_{T}(z)}{z}\right|\geq 2. 

 Note that s_{1}(T)=n for any tree T of order n. By the reverse triangle inequality and Lemma 4.1 , we have 

 \displaystyle\left|\frac{\varPhi_{T}(z)}{z}\right| 
 \displaystyle=\left|s_{n}(T)z^{n-1}+s_{n-1}(T)z^{n-2}+\cdots+s_{2}(T)z+n\right| 

 \displaystyle\geq n-\left(s_{n}(T)|z|^{n-1}+s_{n-1}(T)|z|^{n-2}+\cdots+s_{2}(T)|z|\right) 

 \displaystyle\geq n-\left(\binom{n-1}{n-1}|z|^{n-1}+\binom{n-1}{n-2}|z|^{n-2}+\cdots+\binom{n-1}{1}|z|+1-1\right) 

 \displaystyle=n+1-(|z|+1)^{n-1} 

 \displaystyle\geq 2. 

 This completes the proof.
∎ 

 We examine the sharpness of the upper and lower bounds on the modulus of subtree roots. In this section,
for any integer n\geq 2, define

 a_{n}=(n-1)^{1/(n-1)}. 

 For the star K_{1,n-1}, we have

 \Phi_{K_{1,n-1}}(z)=z\bigl((z+1)^{n-1}+n-1\bigr). 

 Hence its nonzero subtree roots are

 z_{k}=a_{n}\cos\left(\frac{2k-1}{n-1}\pi\right)-1+\mathrm{i}\cdot a_{n}\sin\left(\frac{2k-1}{n-1}\pi\right),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n-1. 

 We first consider the upper bound in Theorem 1.3. If n is even,
then k=n/2 is an integer and

 z_{n/2}=-1-a_{n}. 

 Clearly, the upper bound in Theorem  1.3 is sharp for every even n.
Now suppose that n is odd, and set

 k=\frac{n-1}{2}. 

 Then

 \frac{(2k-1)\pi}{n-1}=\pi-\frac{\pi}{n-1}, 

 and hence

 z_{k}=-a_{n}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)-1+\mathrm{i}\cdot a_{n}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right). 

 It follows that

 \displaystyle|z_{k}|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=a_{n}^{2}+1+2a_{n}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right) 

 \displaystyle=(1+a_{n})^{2}-2a_{n}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)\right). 

 Therefore,

 \frac{|z_{k}|^{2}}{(1+a_{n})^{2}}=1-\frac{2a_{n}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)\right)}{(1+a_{n})^{2}}. 

 Since
a_{n}\rightarrow 1 and
1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)\rightarrow 0 as n\to\infty,
we obtain

 \lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
n\ {\rm odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{|z_{k}|^{2}}{(1+a_{n})^{2}}=1, 

 and consequently

 \lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
n\ {\rm odd}\end{subarray}}\frac{|z_{k}|}{1+a_{n}}=1. 

 Thus the upper bound in Theorem 1.3 is asymptotically sharp as
n\to\infty through odd integers.

 We next consider the lower bound in Theorem 4.2. Taking k=1, we
have

 z_{1}=a_{n}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)-1+\mathrm{i}\cdot a_{n}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right). 

 Then

 \displaystyle|z_{1}|^{2} 
 \displaystyle=a_{n}^{2}+1-2a_{n}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right) 

 \displaystyle=(a_{n}-1)^{2}+2a_{n}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)\right), 

 and

 \frac{|z_{1}|^{2}}{(a_{n}-1)^{2}}=1+\frac{2a_{n}\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n-1}\right)\right)}{(a_{n}-1)^{2}}. 

 A direct calculation gives

 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|z_{1}|^{2}}{(a_{n}-1)^{2}}=1, 

 and consequently

 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|z_{1}|}{a_{n}-1}=1. 

 Hence the lower bound in Theorem 4.2 is asymptotically sharp.

 5 Concluding remarks

 In this paper, we confirm the conjecture proposed by Brown and Mol.
We also give a lower bound on the modulus” of nonzero complex subtree roots.

 Brown and Mol observed numerically in  [ 4 ] that the
complex subtree roots appear to be distributed approximately around
the point -1/2. They proposed the following conjecture, which
strengthens Theorem 1.3 , and verified it computationally for all
trees of order at most 18.

 Conjecture 5.1 . 

 [ 4 ] 
If T is a tree of order n\geq 2, then the subtree roots of T are contained in the annulus 

 \left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\frac{1}{2}\leq\left|z+\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt[n-1]{n-1}\right\}.

relationship between the bounds proved in this paper and the stronger
translated annulus conjectured by Brown and Mol. We emphasize that
Brown and Mol [ 4 ] carried out more extensive computations for all trees of
order at most 18.

 The method developed in this paper does not appear to extend
directly to Conjecture 5.1 .
A different approach may therefore be required in the future.
